The idea

Give students a reason to ask the question before the philosopher answers it.

Intro courses often run as a survey: Plato one week, Descartes the next, Hume after that. Students meet each argument cold, and the argument has to earn their interest all on its own. And, let's face it, philosophers aren't usually the most captivating writers. For spring 2026 I rebuilt PHIL 115: Philosophical Ideas around a single, semester-long story instead.

Alex Chen, a first-year at Simford University, starts noticing glitches. A squirrel freezes mid-chew for four seconds. A lecture hall stops for everyone except Alex and their roommate. An email from “Architect Support” arrives with one line: Your observant nature has been noted. Alex spends the semester trying to work out what kind of world this is, and what to do about it.

Every week, the students encounter a character confronting a puzzle that we then explore in the week's reading. Alex asks whether a backup of themself would still be them before students read Locke and Parfit on identity. The friends vote on whether to sacrifice fifty-three “instances” to save 3,482 in the week the class reads Mill. A night-shift janitor turns out to have reported the glitches for two years before any student noticed. That's the week we read Fricker on testimonial injustice.

By the time students reach the primary text, they already care how the argument comes out.

The readings didn't change. The story sits in front of the texts and never replaces them. Students still read:

The build

Three collaborators with a clear division of labor.

I chose what to teach and had the last word on every page. Claude was a fast, tireless co-writer. Gemini was the illustrator. Here is the sequence, in the order it happened.

  • Instructor
  • Claude (Anthropic)
  • Gemini “Nano Banana” (Google)
  1. 01
    InstructorClaude

    Fix the syllabus first

    I started with my previous version of the course and a brainstorming session with Claude. We identified the core topics I wanted to cover and the primary sources I wanted students to read: the examined life, skepticism, induction, personal identity, mind, free will, three ethical theories, political authority, epistemic injustice, technology, the problem of evil, and the meaning of life.

    The readings were mostly ones I've taught for years, so the story had to serve the syllabus, not the other way round.

    What went in
    • A list of 13 weekly themes that were brainstormed with Claude based on previous versions of the course
    • The primary text for each week, from Plato's Apology to Wolf's Meaning in Life
    • The prompt: build the course as a story (ok, it was more complex than that)
  2. 02
    InstructorClaude

    Brainstorm the arc

    In conversation with Claude, we worked out the premise (a university that might be a simulation, run by an AI called the Architect), a cast of five students and a professor, and a season outline in which each plot turn lands on that week's reading.

    The outline came back as a storyboard: a theme, a reading, a sequence of beats, and a closing hook for every week. The hooks mattered most: each episode ends on a question that's still open when class begins. I tweaked the storyboard until I was happy with the full arc.

    From the brainstorming conversation

    Claude“Awesome, let's turn this into a proper season outline.”

    Week 1 hook, as outlined“Sets tone, introduces Architect & characters, has tiny glitch that can be nothing… or not.”

    Week 4 hook, as outlined“Makes Locke & Parfit immediate: do previous Alexes' decisions bind this one?”

  3. 03
    Claude draftsInstructor revises

    Write the episodes

    Claude drafted each weekly vignette from the storyboard, roughly 900 words apiece. I revised every draft before students saw it, checking that the philosophy was accurate and that each episode set up the questions I wanted to discuss in class.

    Every episode closes with a page from Alex's notebook. Those entries became a model for the students' own writing.

    End of Episode 1

    Alex's notebookDay 1. Something froze. Probably nothing.

    Prof. Reyes asked how much of my life I've actually examined. Honest answer: almost none.

    Maybe this is what philosophy is. Noticing the weirdness instead of ignoring it.

  4. 04
    Gemini

    Draw the comics

    Each finished vignette went to Gemini's “Nano Banana” image model with instructions to render the week's story as a comic page and to keep the characters and the campus consistent from week to week.

    It did an okay job. The style held steady all semester. The characters did not always stay themselves, which turned into a lesson of its own (see the glitches).

    Episode 5, “Backups”
  5. 05
    Instructor

    Build the course around it

    In Canvas, each weekly module opens with the comic and the episode, then the readings. Twelve micro-writing assignments put students inside the plot: reconstruct the argument Alex keeps circling, object to the group's vote, diagnose what was done to Elena.

    The story gave every assignment a concrete case, so students spent less time inventing examples and more time on the argument.

The glitches

The illustrator had its own personal-identity problem.

Gemini kept the look of the comic steady all semester: the line work, the palette, Alex's brown jacket. It was far less reliable about who was who. Characters drifted between weeks, details from the script went missing, and the lettering sometimes fell apart. I left the slips in.

Weeks 1 → 6 → 8 → 14

Prof. Reyes becomes four people

Week 1 introduces her as a Black woman in her forties with silver-streaked locs. In Week 6 the script has “Professor Reyes” lecturing on d'Holbach, and the comic draws a gray-haired white man in a tie. She's a woman again in Week 8, but younger, with dark curls and no locs. By the final episode, where she turns out to be a researcher in base reality, she has short gray hair.

Weeks 7 → 8

Marcus Webb gets younger

In Week 7 the Architect shows the friends a photo of Marcus, the janitor, as a gray-haired older man. A week later he's back at work as a young blond man.

Weeks 1 → 9 → 13

Maya loses her braids

Week 1 gives her red box braids and a JUSTICE pin. The person holding her megaphone in Week 9 looks like someone else, and Week 13 brings a third version.

Week 4

Dr. Hayes changes gender

The script gives the counselor a name tag and her pronouns. The comic draws a middle-aged man.

Weeks 3, 8, 9, 11

The lettering breaks down

“Jordan readed win't disbelief?” is a whole speech balloon in Week 8, beside an enrollment agreement written in near-English. Protest signs read RIIGHTS FOR USS and PROGGED NOW!, and Alex's phone shows a field labeled “Isobject.”

Week 14

The same line, twice

In the final episode, a researcher in base reality wonders aloud whether they're crossing lines. Gemini put that balloon in the panel twice.

Why I kept them

A fallible illustrator suited this course.

Week 4 asks what makes someone the same person over time. Here was a running example: if Maya looks different in every appearance, what makes her Maya? Her name, the role she plays in the story, her memories, the megaphone? Students had Locke's question in front of them every week, in the course materials themselves.

Week 11 is about AI systems that reproduce the patterns they were trained on and fail people in ways nobody intended. The comics showed a capable model doing most of the job well and getting the details wrong in ways a careful reader catches. Importantly, students noticed that the race of the characters may have contributed to the glitches and tied this back to potential problems with the training data. That's a fair picture of where these tools are, and students saw it firsthand instead of hearing it from me.

The writing

Assignments set inside the story.

Twelve micro-writings of 400 to 600 words, each practicing one named skill and each built on a moment from an episode.

Every assignment is graded on a short specification checklist rather than points. A submission either meets the specs or gets a path to revision:

✓Meets expectations. Credit earned.
✓−Almost there. One revision allowed within a week.
✗Not yet. Revision required for any credit.

After class each week, students also file a 150–250 word discussion log in three parts: what I contributed, what I learned, and what I'm still thinking about. It's a close cousin of Alex's notebook.

The last micro-writing hands students the pen. They write a page of Alex's notebook from any moment in the story, in Alex's voice, explaining a philosophical concept in Alex's words.

Try it yourself

What I'd tell a colleague starting this in their own course.

Start from the reading list, and make every plot turn land on a text.

The story has one job: to get students to the primary source with a question already in hand. Decide the readings and their order first. Then ask what would have to happen to a character for that reading to feel urgent that week. If an episode is fun but doesn't set up the text, cut it or rewrite it.

Use the AI as a co-writer, not the author.

Claude drafted quickly and took direction, but every episode went through my revision. Check the philosophy hardest: a character's paraphrase of Kant becomes what students think Kant said.

End each episode on a question.

Alex's notebook gave every week a closing prompt, so discussion could start from a question the story had left open.

Give the image model a character sheet, every time.

An image model only knows what is in the request in front of it. Paste the same physical descriptions into every prompt, and attach earlier pages as references if your tool allows it.

Check every panel before you post it.

Look for faces, names, pronouns, and lettering. Even with good prompts, expect drift. Decide in advance which slips you'll fix and which you'll keep and talk about.

Keep the important words out of the art.

Image models still misspell. Anything students need to read closely belongs in the episode text, with the comic as a companion to it.

Tell students how the materials were made.

Say which parts were drafted by AI and which by you. In a course with a week on AI ethics, the disclosure is part of the lesson.
